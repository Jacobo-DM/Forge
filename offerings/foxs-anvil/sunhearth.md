# SunHearth

*A Fox’s Anvil offering from Professor Mitsyfibbins.*

## Story Envelope

Professor Mitsyfibbins did not make the SunHearth because she wanted a better heater.

She made it because too many rooms had forgotten how to gather around a small shared warmth without turning it into a machine, a product, or a performance.

In one telling, it began in the old Moonfruit days, when the fruit no longer tasted quite right. People argued about ingredients, measurements, and factory parts. Mitsyfibbins suspected the trouble was deeper. The problem was not only with the fruit. It was with forgetting.

So she began again, as she often did, with something small enough to notice.

A little flame.  
A shaping bowl.  
A watching stone.  

Not enough to heat a house.  
Enough to gather attention.

The SunHearth became one of the early offerings of the Fox’s Anvil: a tiny sun cradled in a bowl, made for supervised inquiry, reflection, and warmth shared through presence rather than scale.

It asks a simple question:

**What changes when warmth is tended instead of consumed?**

## What This Offering Is

The **SunHearth** is a model world in miniature — a tiny sun cradled in a bowl — used to help students see and feel how warmth moves across shapes, surfaces, and stories.

It is not offered as a home-heating device, survival tool, or technical build plan.

It is a teaching object, story object, and attention object.

## The Three Parts

Every SunHearth rests on three simple principles:

### The Little Flame

The seed of warmth.

### The Shaping Bowl

The horizon that gathers, reflects, and redirects.

### The Watching Stone

The presence that listens.

Placed together with care, they form a small universe:

**sun, earth, sky.**

## What It Helps Teach

In a Circle of Warmth lesson, students might use the SunHearth to explore:

- how heat travels by radiation, conduction, and convection
- how curved shapes guide light, sound, and warmth
- how surfaces absorb, reflect, or redirect energy
- how temperature and reflection can be observed and measured
- how ancestors survived cold seasons with ingenuity
- how a small tended flame can become a shared center

## How To Use This Offering

Use the SunHearth slowly.

Begin with observation before explanation.

Let students notice what changes:

- Where does the warmth go?
- What does the bowl gather?
- What does the stone seem to do?
- What happens when people move closer or farther away?
- What can be measured?
- What can only be felt?

The point is not to rush toward a correct answer.

The point is to let attention gather around warmth.

## How Not To Use This Offering

Do not present the SunHearth as a practical heating device.

Do not invite unsupervised flame work.

Do not turn it into a product pitch, survival hack, or classroom spectacle.

Do not let the technical explanation arrive before the students have had time to notice.

The SunHearth belongs to listening, tending, and inquiry.

## Symbol Key

When drawing or embossing the SunHearth for students, this simple symbol language may be used:

**Flame**  
A small upward curve or teardrop at the center — the Little Flame.

**Bowl**  
A wide, shallow arc beneath the flame — the Shaping Bowl, like a horizon.

**Dome**  
A soft arc above the flame — the reflecting sky or hood.

**Stone**  
A circle resting at the top — the Watching Stone, guardian of the flame.

**Circle**  
A faint ring around everything — the gathering of people and stories.

## Safety Note

It is not a guide for building or using heating devices in homes, shelters, camps, or emergency settings.
