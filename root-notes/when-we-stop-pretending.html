Forge Root Note / Threshold Essay

When We Stop Pretending

Shared freely. Creative Commons. January 2026.

This is not a summary of the Forge, a DiscoveryMode pitch, or an education reform proposal.

It is a place to stand before entering the work.

Education has been sold to the poor as a way out of poverty.

And for many, it has worked—no doubt.

But its architecture is extractive.

It is a system. Inputs. Processes. Outputs.

The input is you.

You move through its processes—assessed, sorted, credentialed—and the machine hopes that you will fit into an industry-approved hole. It may be one of comfort or one of precarity. To those who tend the machine, the distinction matters very little. They know the gears. They hold the keys. They count their coins while you go hungry.

Across the world, teachers freely share lesson plans—thoughtful, alive, humane. Materials that could light up classrooms everywhere. Many of them would give their work freely, gladly. But these offerings are crushed under the weight of compliance, content mandates, and sheer exhaustion. Rent. Childcare. Groceries. Medical bills. Survival.

Let’s be honest.

No one enters education thinking they are going to strike it rich.

So what’s going on? For once, let’s actually pause and consider it.

Something changes the moment we stop pretending. When we ask—honestly—what is true and what is false, the scaffolding begins to shake.

Because what is true is this: when learning is freed from extraction, coercion, and performance, people light up—no matter the age.

When I suggest to teachers and students that we gather at Mile Rock Beach instead of a classroom, the response is electric. When I ask students—of any age—whether they would like to travel to Monterey Bay to learn about kelp forests, whales, and marine ecology while learning to scuba dive, they don’t hesitate. They ignite.

Why?

Because this is what we are when we are allowed to be human.

Curious.
Free.
Connected.

Learning happens in these environments—not as performance, not as compliance, not as credential manufacture, but as real encounter.

Knowledge earned with the body, with breath, with weather, with risk, with care.

I would challenge any serious educator to prove otherwise. Not with policy, not with paperwork—but with lived experience.

The arguments against this way of learning are always the same. They arrive dressed as pragmatism, but they all trace back to the same roots: liability, standardization, control, fear.

These are not educational principles.

They are the operating language of extractive systems.

Our children do not need more polished cages.

They do not need better metrics for forgetting what they already know.

They deserve environments that honor their humanity. And the uncomfortable truth—for institutions, but a liberating one for people—is that we already have everything we need to make this real.

I don’t call this an alternative to education. I don’t call it a program. I’ve come to think of it as a way of noticing—what happens when learning is allowed to emerge from place, movement, shared attention, and care. I’ve been calling this orientation DiscoveryMode, not as a solution, but as a reminder. A small practice of staying curious. Of letting the body, the land, and the moment teach first.

Nothing here is proprietary. Nothing needs permission.

Maybe the work now is not to fix the machine, but to stop feeding it. To walk to the edge of the land. To stand where systems lose their grip and learning remembers its natural habitat.

This is not a proposal.

It is a place you can stand.
